Big gaps don’t just look unfair — they change people’s options.

Income inequality is the gap between people who earn a lot and people who earn a little. When that gap grows, it affects housing, health, education, and how confident people feel about the future.

Quick glossary

  • Income = money earned (jobs + benefits + other income).
  • Disposable income = income after taxes + transfers.
  • Wealth (net worth) = what you own minus what you owe.
  • Low income = below a threshold (Canada often uses LIM-AT).
How does Canada define “income gap”?

Statistics Canada defines the income gap as the difference in the share of disposable income between the top 40% and the bottom 40% of households.

Income inequality vs wealth inequality

Income is what comes in each year. Wealth is what builds up over years (or generations). Wealth inequality is often larger because assets can grow over time.

What inequality actually means

Inequality isn’t automatically “bad” just because people earn different amounts. It becomes a serious issue when the gap is so large that it changes what people can realistically do: afford housing, access education, stay healthy, or recover from emergencies.

Fair chance problem

When the starting line is wildly different, effort alone doesn’t decide outcomes.

Stability problem

One surprise bill can push low-income households into debt or homelessness.

Trust problem

If people think the system is “rigged,” social trust and cooperation drop.

Myth vs fact

Myth: “Inequality doesn’t matter if the economy grows.”

Fact: Growth can concentrate at the top. That’s why we look at how income is shared across groups (like top 40% vs bottom 40%).

Myth: “It’s only about income.”

Fact: Wealth (assets and debts) can be even more uneven — and it compounds over time.

Myth: “Low income is the same as being lazy.”

Fact: Low income is measured using thresholds like LIM-AT, and many people in low income are working.

Why these measures are used

This isn’t “vibes.” It’s measurement. Governments and researchers track inequality to understand who’s gaining, who’s losing, and whether policy is helping.

Income gap (top 40% vs bottom 40%)

This measures how much disposable income is concentrated with higher-income households compared to lower-income households. It’s easy to explain and updated frequently in household economic accounts.

StatCan definition + context

Low Income Measure, after tax (LIM-AT)

LIM-AT is a relative measure based on a share of median adjusted household income (so it moves as society changes).

StatCan — Canadian Income Survey 2023

Wealth gap (top 20% vs bottom 40%)

This tracks how net worth is distributed. It’s especially important because wealth can grow (or shrink) through markets, housing prices, and interest rates.

Who gets hit hardest?

  • Young people starting out: costs (especially housing) hit first.
  • Low-wage / unstable workers: less predictable income = more risk.
  • Single-parent households: higher costs with fewer buffers.
  • Some groups face barriers: discrimination and uneven access make gaps stick.

Real-life effects

  • Housing choices shrink → long commutes, overcrowding, or insecurity.
  • Health stress increases → mental load, less preventative care.
  • Education pathways narrow → fewer opportunities over time.
One sentence: inequality isn’t a jealousy problem — it’s an options problem.

Why inequality grows

Wages vs costs

If essentials rise faster than pay, the bottom falls behind.

Different pay ceilings

Some careers scale; others don’t, even if they’re essential jobs.

Compounding wealth

Assets can grow over time (markets/housing), widening gaps.

Important: These causes can stack. Someone can be working hard and still lose ground if costs outrun income.

What Canada says it’s aiming for

Canada’s SDG 10 ambition includes reducing inequalities and ensuring people live free of discrimination.

Canada.ca — Sustainable Development Goal 10: Reduced inequalities

What tends to help (high level)

  • More affordable housing supply + protections
  • Skills pathways into stable work (training + apprenticeships)
  • Policies that reduce child poverty
  • Reducing discrimination and barriers
Reality check: no single policy fixes it. Progress is usually a bundle of changes over time.

FAQ (fast answers)

Is inequality the same as poverty?

Not exactly. Poverty is about falling below a threshold (like LIM-AT). Inequality is about how unevenly income/wealth is distributed.

Why do we care if the “average” is fine?

Averages can hide huge gaps. Distribution stats (like top 40% vs bottom 40%) show whether gains are shared.
