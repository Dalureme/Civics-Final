Income inequality changes what people can realistically do.

Income inequality is the gap between people who earn a lot and people who earn a little. When the gap grows, it affects affordability, stability, and opportunity.

Facts + sources
Plain explanations
Real actions
Main idea: if essential costs rise faster than income for a lot of people, the gap grows even if the overall economy is improving.

Key terms

  • Income = money earned (jobs + benefits + other income).
  • Disposable income = income after taxes and transfers.
  • Wealth (net worth) = what someone owns minus what they owe.
  • Low income = below a threshold (Canada often uses LIM-AT).
How Statistics Canada defines “income gap”

Statistics Canada defines the income gap as the difference in the share of disposable income between the top 40% and the bottom 40% of households.

Income inequality vs wealth inequality

Income is what comes in each year. Wealth is what builds up over time through savings, assets, and property. Wealth inequality can be larger because assets can grow over time.

What this means

Inequality isn’t just “some people have more money.” It becomes a real problem when the gap is large enough that it changes what people can afford and what opportunities they can access. It also tends to make economic shocks hit harder for people with less savings and fewer supports.

Affordability

When necessities rise faster than wages, people fall behind even if they work.

Stability

With little savings, one unexpected cost can cause debt or housing stress.

Opportunity

Costs and barriers can limit education, mobility, and long-term outcomes.

Myths that don’t hold up

Myth: “If the economy grows, inequality doesn’t matter.”

Reality: growth can concentrate. Distribution measures show whether gains are shared.

Myth: “It’s just about people making bad choices.”

Reality: costs, wages, job security, and access to opportunities all matter.

Myth: “Income and wealth are basically the same.”

Reality: income is what you earn; wealth is what you accumulate (or inherit).

How it’s measured

Income gap (top 40% vs bottom 40%)

This measures how much disposable income is concentrated with higher-income households compared to lower-income households.

Statistics Canada (definition + context)

Low Income Measure, after tax (LIM-AT)

LIM-AT is a relative measure based on median adjusted household income.

Wealth gap (top 20% vs bottom 40%)

A wealth gap measure tracks differences in net worth distribution. It can shift with housing prices, debt, and interest rates.

Who is affected most

  • Young households starting out: rent and necessities can take up most income.
  • Low-wage or unstable workers: fewer benefits and less predictable hours.
  • Single-parent households: higher costs with fewer buffers.
  • Groups facing barriers: discrimination and uneven access can keep gaps in place.

What it looks like

  • Housing stress: less choice, more instability, longer commutes.
  • Health stress: higher pressure and fewer options for prevention.
  • Education limits: fewer resources can narrow paths over time.
Quick summary: inequality is mostly about how many real options people have.

Why inequality grows

Wages vs costs

When necessities rise faster than pay, the gap widens.

Different pay ceilings

Some jobs scale quickly, others stay low even when demand is high.

Compounding wealth

Assets can grow over time; people without assets miss out on that growth.

It’s not one cause. Inequality usually comes from multiple systems stacking together.

What’s being done

Canada’s SDG 10 page describes goals related to reducing inequalities and addressing discrimination.

Canada.ca — Sustainable Development Goal 10: Reduced inequalities

What tends to help

  • Affordable housing supply and stability measures
  • Pathways into stable work (training, apprenticeships, supports)
  • Reducing child poverty and food insecurity
  • Removing barriers and discrimination
Bottom line: lasting improvement usually comes from a bundle of changes, not one single fix.

FAQ

Is inequality the same as poverty?

Not exactly. Poverty (or low income) is about being below a threshold. Inequality is about how unevenly income and wealth are distributed.

Why do we care if an average looks fine?

Averages can hide gaps. Distribution measures show whether gains are shared across groups.
